The main purely mathematical property of linear transformations is that they preserve the structure of vector spaces. This means that if we have three vectors $\vec{a},\ \vec{b}$ and $\vec{c}$ such that $\vec{c}=\alpha\vec{a}+\beta\vec{b}$, then for any linear transformation $T$,
\begin{equation}
	T(\vec{c}) = \alpha T(\vec{a}) + \beta T(\vec{b}).
	\label{eq:}
\end{equation}
(see \autoref{fig:linear trans preservering linear structure} for a visual example)

However, this is a rather abstract way of discussing linear transformations. Instead, let's first learn some of the more \enquote{graphical} properies of linear transformations in the plane, i.e. $T:\Rs[2]\to\Rs[2]$, and then see practical examples of such transformations before moving on to their more abstract (and powerful) mathematical description.

Any linear transformation $T:\Rs[2]\to\Rs[2]$ always has the following four properties:
\begin{descitemize}
	\item[The origin is kept untouched] $T$ always maps $\vec{0}$ to $\vec{0}$.
	\item[Lines remain lines] a set of points forming a line in $\Rs[2]$ will be mapped to another set of points forming a line (with possibly different direction, distance from origin and scale).
	\item[Parallel directions remain parallel] if $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ point at the same direction, then $\vec{b}=\alpha\vec{a}$ for some $\alpha\in\Rs$. After the transformation the maps of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ will stay parallels, and would even keep their scalar relation: $T(\vec{b})=\alpha T(\vec{a})$.
	\item[All areas are scaled by the same factor] if two shapes $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ have areas $A_{1}$ and $A_{2}$, respectively, before the transformation is applied, their maps $\tilde{S}_{1}$ and $\tilde{S}_{2}$ would have the areas $kA_{1}$ and $kA_{2}$ (where $k\in\Rs$ depends on the transformation and can take both positive, zero and negative values).
\end{descitemize}
\autoref{fig:general linear transformation and invariants} shows these properties in action: there are two planes in the figure - before and after the application of a random linear transformation $T$ (up and down, respectively). The following can be seen:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The origin stays at the same place - see how a small purple dot placed on the origin doesn't change its position after the application of $T$.
	\item All lines (e.g. axes, grid lines, the two purple lines) are mapped to lines in the transformed plane.
	\item Parallel directions remain parallel, e.g. grid lines aligned with one of the axes, and the two purple lines.
	\item All areas are scaled by the same factor: notice for example how all unit squares formed by the grid lines (one is highlighted in orange) are mapped to identical parallelograms. You can also check to see that the blue rectangle is exactly twice as large than the orange square before and after the transformation.
\end{enumerate}

In the original unit square the diagonals are equal: their lengths are $\textcolor{xorange}{\mathbf{OB}}=\textcolor{xorange}{\mathbf{AC}}=\sqrt{2}$. However, it's clear that $\textcolor{xorange}{\mathbf{O'B'}} < \textcolor{xorange}{\mathbf{A'C'}}$. In addition, all the angles in the original unit square are, of course, right angles, but the transformed square is a parallelogram ($\textcolor{xorange}{\mathbf{O'A'B'C'}}$) has two equal acute angles of value $\alpha$ and two equal obtuse angles of value $\beta$ (where $\beta=\pi-\alpha$).

So we can see that at least two quantities aren't necessarily conserved by linear transformations: (1) \textbf{distances}, which unlike areas don't even all scale by the same amount, and (2) \textbf{angles} (more generally, \textbf{directions}).

One last key observation before we move on: as can be seen in \autoref{fig:general linear transformation and invariants} and \autoref{fig:orange unit square in R2} linear transfornations in $\Rs[2]$ transform \textbf{squares} into \textbf{parallelograms}. These parallelograms can be scaled, \enquote{squeezed}, reflected and/or rotated compared to the original squares.

\subsection{Formal Definition}
The formal definition of linear transformations between any two vector spaces is the following:

\begin{definition}{Linear Transformation}{linear_trans}
	A transformation $T:\Rs[n]\to\Rs[m]$ is said to be \textit{linear} if for any two vectors $\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\Rs[n]$ and any scalar $\alpha\in\Rs$:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item $T\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right) = T(\vec{a})+T(\vec{b})$, and
		\item $T\left(\alpha \vec{a}\right) = \alpha T(\vec{a})$.
	\end{enumerate}

	We can combine the above two criteria using an additional scalar $\beta\in\Rs$: $T$ is linear if $$ T(\alpha\vec{a} + \beta\vec{b}) = \alpha T(\vec{a}) + \beta T(\vec{b}).$$
\end{definition}

Just like with vectirs, the first condition is called \textbf{additivity} and the second condition is called \textbf{homogeneity}. What these conditions say, in simple words, is that we can always scale vectors and add them together before we apply the a linear transformation - and the result will be equal to scaling and adding the transformed vectors. So in practice we can choose what we want to calculate first (addition and scaling or the transformation) according to what is easier and/or more convinient to calculate.

Let's see now how to use this definition to show that a transformation is linear. We can start with the identity transformation since it's a very simple transformation.

\begin{descitemize}
	\item[Additivity] for any $\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\Rs[2]$, we have
	\begin{equation}
		I(\vec{a})=\vec{a},\ I(\vec{b})=\vec{b},
		\label{eq:identity trans in R2 acting on two vectors}
	\end{equation}
	and also that
	\begin{equation}
		I\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right)=\vec{a}+\vec{b}.
		\label{eq:identity trans in R2 acting on addition of vectors}
	\end{equation}
	Therefore, if we first add the vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ together and then apply $I$ on the result, we get

	\vspace{0.5em}
	\begin{equation}
		I\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right)\ \tikzmarknode{Iadd}{=}\ \vec{a}+\vec{b}\ \tikzmarknode{Itrans}{=}\ I(\vec{a})+I(\vec{b}).
		\label{eq:}
	\end{equation}
	\begin{tikzpicture}[remember picture]
		\small

		\node[overlay, eqtxbox={xgreen}, yshift=10mm] (IaddTxt) at (pic cs:Iadd) {from \autoref{eq:identity trans in R2 acting on addition of vectors}};
		\draw[overlay, eqtxboxarrow={xgreen}, shorten >=8pt] (IaddTxt.south) -- (pic cs:Iadd);

		\node[overlay, eqtxbox={xred}, yshift=-8mm] (ItransTxt) at (pic cs:Itrans) {from \autoref{eq:identity trans in R2 acting on two vectors}};
		\draw[overlay, eqtxboxarrow={xred}, shorten >=2pt] (ItransTxt.north) -- (pic cs:Itrans);
	\end{tikzpicture}

	\vspace{0.5em}
	\item[Homogeneity] for any vector $\vec{a}\in\Rs[2]$ and scalar $\alpha\in\Rs$ we have
	\begin{equation}
		I\left(\alpha\vec{a}\right) = \alpha\vec{a},
		\label{eq:}
	\end{equation}
	and together with \autoref{eq:identity trans in R2 acting on two vectors} (specifically its first equality) we get
	\begin{equation}
		I\left(\alpha\vec{a}\right) = \alpha\vec{a} = \alpha I(\vec{a}).
		\label{eq:}
	\end{equation}
\end{descitemize}
Fullfilling these two conditions proves that $I$ is indeed a linear transformation.

Next, let's see that rotation (about the origin) is also a linear transformation, starting with the simple $\ang{90}$ anti-clockwise rotation. Recall that a 2-dimensional vector $\vec{a}=\GenericColVecXY{a}$ can be rotated by $\ang{90}$ anti-clockwise by exchanging its components and then adding a minus sign to the first component (REF TO TRICK):
\begin{equation*}
	\GenericColVecXY{a} \xmapsto[\text{(anti-clockwise)}]{\ang{90}\ \text{rotation}} \colvec{-a_{y};a_{x}}.
\end{equation*}

Using this fact we can easily show that a $\ang{90}$ rotation is a linear transformation:
\begin{descitemize}
	\item[Additivity] we need two vectors, let's call them $$\vec{a}=\GenericColVecXY{a}\ \text{and}\ \vec{b}=\GenericColVecXY{b}.$$ Then, adding the vectors first and then rotating the result we get
	\begin{equation*}
		\RotDeg{90}{\vec{a}+\vec{b}} = \RotDeg{90}{\GenericColVecXY{a}+\GenericColVecXY{b}}
		= \RotDeg{90}{\colvec{a_{x}+b_{x};a_{y}+b_{y}}}
		= \colvec{-\left(a_{y}+b_{y}\right);a_{x}+b_{x}}.
	\end{equation*}
	And if we first rotate the vectors and then add the result we get
	\begin{equation*}
		\RotDeg{90}{\vec{a}} + \RotDeg{90}{\vec{b}} = \colvec{-a_{y};a_{x}}+\colvec{-b_{y};b_{x}} = \colvec{-(a_{y}+b_{y});a_{x}+b_{x}},
	\end{equation*}
	exactly the same as before.

	\item[Homogeneity] if we scale $\vec{a}$ by some scalar\footnote{I'm using $s$ here instead of the usual $\alpha$ because I want to avoid confusion the scalar with an angle.} $s\in\Rs$ first and then rotating the result we get
	\begin{equation*}
		\RotDeg{90}{s\vec{a}} = \RotDeg{90}{s\colvec{-a_{y};a_{x}}} = \RotDeg{90}{\GenericColVecXY{s a}} = \colvec{-s a_{y};s a_{x}}.
	\end{equation*}
	And if we first rotate $\vec{a}$ and then scale it by $s$ we get
	\begin{equation*}
		s \RotDeg{90}{\vec{a}} = s \colvec{-a_{y};a_{x}} = \colvec{-s a_{y};s a_{x}},
	\end{equation*}
	again - the same result as before.
\end{descitemize}
Together this means that rotating around the origin anti-clockwise by $\ang{90}$ is indeed a linear transformation.

Moving on, let's show that a rotation around the origin by \textit{any} angle $\theta$ is a linear transformation. To start, we need to know how to transform a general 2-dimensional vector $\vec{a}=\GenericColVecXY{a}$, which we will find, of course, by using basic trigonometry (use \autoref{fig:rotation of a generic vector in R2} to follow these steps). First, we calculate the norm of $\vec{a}$ (which we'll call $\mathbf{a}$ for convenience):
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{a} = \norm{\vec{a}} = \sqrt{a_{x}^{2}+a_{y}^{2}}.
	\label{eq:norm of a generic 2D vector a}
\end{equation}

We can then express $\vec{a}$ in polar coordinates (REF), as it's a more natural way of dealing with angles (and thus - rotations):
\begin{align}
	a_{x} &= \mathbf{a}\cos(\alpha),\\
	a_{y} &= \mathbf{a}\sin(\alpha).
	\label{eq:vector a in polar coords}
\end{align}

Rotating the $\vec{a}$ by $\theta$ yields a new vector $\vec{b}$ which as the same norm as $\vec{a}$ (since rotating a vector doesn't change its norm), and an angle $\beta=\alpha+\theta$. Therefore in polar coordinates $\vec{b}$ is
\begin{align}
	b_{x} &= \mathbf{a}\cos(\beta) = \mathbf{a}\cos(\alpha+\theta),\\
	b_{y} &= \mathbf{a}\sin(\beta) = \mathbf{a}\sin(\alpha+\theta).
	\label{eq:vector b in polar coords}
\end{align}

Using the trigonomitric identities for the $cos$ and $sin$ of a sum of two angles (REF), we get:
\begin{align}
	b_{x} &= \mathbf{a}\cos(\alpha+\theta) = \mathbf{a}\left[\cos(\alpha)\cos(\theta) - \sin(\alpha)\sin(\theta)\right],\\
	b_{y} &= \mathbf{a}\sin(\alpha+\theta) = \mathbf{a}\left[\sin(\alpha)\cos(\theta) + \sin(\alpha)\cos(\theta)\right].\\
	\label{eq:vector b in polar coords}
\end{align}

Now, we can express $\alpha$ in terms of the inverse trigonometric functions $\arccos$ and $\arcsin$:
\begin{equation}
	\alpha = \arccos(\frac{a_{x}}{\mathbf{a}}) = \arcsin(\frac{a_{y}}{\mathbf{a}}).
	\label{eq:angle alpha in terms of arccos and arcsin}
\end{equation}

Substituting \autoref{eq:angle alpha in terms of arccos and arcsin} into \autoref{eq:vector b in polar coords} we get
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	b_{x} &= \mathbf{a}\left[\cos(\alpha)\cos(\theta) - \sin(\alpha)\sin(\theta)\right]\\
		  &= \mathbf{a}\left[\cos\left(\arccos\left(\frac{a_{x}}{\mathbf{a}}\right)\right)\cos(\theta) - \sin\left(\arcsin\left(\frac{a_{y}}{\mathbf{a}}\right)\right)\sin(\theta)\right]\\
		  &= \cancel{\mathbf{a}}\left[\frac{a_{x}}{\cancel{\mathbf{a}}}\cos(\theta) - \frac{a_{y}}{\cancel{\mathbf{a}}}\sin(\theta)\right]\\
		  &= a_{x}\cos(\theta)-a_{y}\sin(\theta),
\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	b_{y} &= \mathbf{a}\left[\sin(\alpha)\cos(\theta) - \sin(\alpha)\cos(\theta)\right]\\
		  &= \mathbf{a}\left[\sin\left(\arcsin\left(\frac{a_{y}}{\mathbf{a}}\right)\right)\cos(\theta) - \sin\left(\arcsin\left(\frac{a_{y}}{\mathbf{a}}\right)\right)\cos(\theta)\right]\\
		  &= \cancel{\mathbf{a}}\left[\frac{a_{y}}{\cancel{\mathbf{a}}}\cos(\theta) - \frac{a_{x}}{\cancel{\mathbf{a}}}\sin(\theta)\right]\\
		  &= a_{x}\sin(\theta)+a_{y}\cos(\theta),
\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:}
\end{equation}

\subsection{3-Dimensional Linear Transformations}

\subsection{Calculating Linear Transformations}
Linear transformations have a very nice property which makes applying them on different vectors an extremely efficient process. Consider the linear transformation $T:\Rs[2]\to\Rs[2]$ which is a composition of scaling by a factor of $2$ in the $y$-diretion followed by a rotation of $\ang{90}$ anti-clockwise around the origin. Now let's say that we wish to calculate how $T$ transforms the vector
$$\vec{a}=\colvec{3;2}.$$

In principle, we could simply apply the operations one after the other on $\vec{a}$:
\begin{equation}
	\vec{a} = \colvec{3;2} \xmapsto{\text{$\times 2$ in $y$}} \colvec{3;4} \xmapsto{\text{$\ang{90}$ rotation}} \colvec{-4;3} = T(\vec{a}).
	\label{eq:simple_LT_example_single_2d_vec}
\end{equation}

Now, what happens if we wish to calculate a more complicated linear transformation, which is both composed out of many more basic transformations - and also acts on higher-dimensional vectors? Performing each transformation step might not be so straight-forward, and is definitely not an efficient process. In fact, even if we're dealing with a simple example just like the one we just saw, we would still need to apply it every time we want to calculate how a new vector is transformed.

It turns out that thanks to the simplicity of linear transformations \footnote{more correctly: to the strict rules they enforce} we only need to perform a few simple calculations per transformation to practically get any possible application of it on any vector!

To see how this is the case, let's go back to our simple $2$-dimensional example. First, recall that we can express any vector in terms of the standard basis vectors (REF), which in this case gives us (note the color-coding)
\begin{equation}
	\vec{a} = \clrcolvecxy{3}{2} = \LCa{3}\overset{\substack{\ebx \\ \downarrow}}{\colvec{1;0}} + \LCb{2}\overset{\substack{\eby \\ \downarrow}}{\colvec{0;1}}.
	\label{eq:vec a representation in the standard basis}
\end{equation}

We can then apply the defining properties of linear transformations (REF) on $\vec{a}$:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		T\clrcolvecxy{3}{2} & = T\left(\LCa{3}\colvec{1;0}+\LCb{2}\colvec{0;1}\right)                 \\
		                    & = T\left(\LCa{3}\colvec{1;0}\right) + T\left(\LCb{2}\colvec{0;1}\right) \\
		                    & = \LCa{3}T\colvec{1;0} + \LCb{2}T\colvec{0;1}                           \\
		                    & = \LCa{3}T\left(\ebx\right) + \LCb{2}T\left(\eby\right).
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:T applied to a in std basis representation}
\end{equation}

Now, applying $T$ to the two standard basis vectors $\ebx$ and $\eby$ we get
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\ebx & = \colvec{1;0} \xmapsto[\text{$\times 2$ in $y$}]{} \colvec{1;0} \xmapsto[\text{$\ang{90}$ rotation}]{} \colvec{0;1} = T(\ebx)                                    \\
		\eby & = \colvec{0;1} \xmapsto{\textcolor{white}{\text{$\times 2$ in $y$}}} \colvec{0;2} \xmapsto{\textcolor{white}{\text{$\ang{90}$ rotation}}} \colvec{-2;0} = T(\eby)
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:simple LT example transformed basis vectors}
\end{equation}

And when we use these transformed basis vectors in the linear combination we finally get
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\LCa{3}T\left(\ebx\right) + \LCb{2}T\left(\eby\right) & = \LCa{3}\colvec{0;1} +\LCb{2}\colvec{-2;0} \\
		                                                      & = \colvec{0;3} + \colvec{-4;0}              \\
		                                                      & = \colvec{-4;3},
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:}
\end{equation}
which is exactly what we got when we calculated $T$'s effect directly on $\vec{a}$ (cf. \autoref{eq:simple_LT_example_single_2d_vec}).

Notice exactly what happened: in the standard basis representation, $\vec{a}$ had the coefficients $\LCa{3}$ and $\LCb{2}$ for $\ebx$ and $\eby$, respectively. When applying $T$ to $\vec{a}$ these two coefficients remained, but the basis changed: instead of $\left\{\ebx,\eby\right\}$, the two basis vectors became $\left\{T(\ebx),T(\eby)\right\}$.

Of course, this is not unique to $\vec{a}$. For example, let's look at another vector, $$\vec{b}=\clrcolvecxy{2}{-4}.$$ Its coefficients in the standard basis representation are $\LCa{2}$ and $\LCb{-4}$, respectively. So we expect $T(\vec{b})$ to be
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		T(\vec{b}) & = \LCa{2}T(\ebx) - \LCb{4}T(\eby)            \\
		           & = \LCa{2}\colvec{0;1} - \LCb{4}\colvec{-2;0} \\
		           & = \colvec{0;2} + \colvec{8;0}                \\
		           & = \colvec{8;2}.
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:second_simple_LT_example_single_2d_vec}
\end{equation}

If we now calculate $T(\vec{b})$ directly, we get the following:
\begin{equation}
	\vec{b} = \colvec{2;-4} \xmapsto{\text{$\times 2$ in $y$}} \colvec{2;-8} \xmapsto{\text{$\ang{90}$ rotation}} \colvec{8;2} = T(\vec{b}).
	\label{eq:simple_LT_example_single_2d_vec}
\end{equation}

What this insight shows us is that given a linear transformation, we only need to know how it transforms the respective standard basis vectors in order to calculate how it transform any vector in its domain. The transformed version of the vector would simply be the linear combination of these transformed basis vectors, with the coefficients given by the vector's presentation in the standard basis. It really can't get any simpler than that!

To make this a bit more formal, let's look at the most generic case: say $T:\Rs[n]\to\Rs[n]$ is a linear transformation \footnote{we'll deal with the more general case of $T:\Rs[n]\to\Rs[m]$ where $n\neq m$ in the next section.}. Then applying $T$ to a vector $\vec{v}\in\Rs[n]$ in its standard basis representation, $$\vec{v}=\clrgenericvec{v}{n},$$ we get
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		T(\vec{v}) & = T\clrgenericvec{v}{n}                                                         \\
		           & = T\left( \LCa{v_{1}}\ebx + \LCb{v_{2}}\eby + \dots + \LCe{v_{n}}\eb{n} \right) \\
		           & = \LCa{v_{1}}T(\ebx) + \LCb{v_{2}}T(\eby) + \dots + \LCe{v_{n}}T(\eb{n}).
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:}
\end{equation}
